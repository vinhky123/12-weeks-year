# The 12 Week Year — Verified Methodology Reference & App Mapping

> **Scope and limits:** This is a secondary summary and implementation reference for *The 12 Week Year*, not the book's full text or a substitute for a specific edition. It combines the authors' framework, editorial explanation, and app-specific suggestions; those are labeled separately where relevant. Chapter titles are checked against the 2013 book's published table of contents. Numerical recommendations and memorable claims should be treated as the authors' or guide's heuristics unless a source is cited; they are not guarantees of results.

- **Book:** *The 12 Week Year: Get More Done in 12 Weeks than Others Do in 12 Months*
- **Authors:** Brian P. Moran & Michael Lennington
- **Publisher:** Wiley, 2013 (expanded editions later)
- **Official site:** https://12weekyear.com

---

## 1. The Core Thesis: Execution, Not Knowledge, Is the Gap

Moran's starting point: **most people already know what to do** — eat better, make the sales calls, write the book. The gap between where you are and where you want to be is **not a knowledge gap; it is an execution gap**.

- "Knowledge is only powerful when acted on." Applied knowledge is power; unused knowledge is trivia.
- "It's not what you know... it's how well you execute that matters."
- **Effective, consistent execution is the way forward.** The whole book is a system for making execution predictable instead of hoping for motivation.

### 1.1 The Problem: "Annualized Thinking"

The traditional 12-month goal cycle silently sabotages execution:

1. **False sense of urgency-absence.** A December deadline makes January feel like there's plenty of time. Every day feels skippable. "We lack a sense of urgency, not realizing that every week is important, every day is important."
2. **Delayed feedback.** Annual goals give you effectively **one feedback moment** (end of year). By the time you know you're off track, the year is gone. Fast feedback loops drive correction.
3. **Spectator phenomenon.** Early in the year, the deadline is so distant that goals feel optional; you watch the year happen instead of driving it.
4. **Sprawling, unfocused plans.** Annual plans accumulate too many objectives, so none get the concentrated effort they need.
5. The book's argument is about the long delay between setting annual intentions and receiving useful feedback. Popular claims about a specific "Quitters' Day" or the share of people who abandon resolutions are not needed to establish that argument and are omitted here because this reference did not verify a suitable primary source.

### 1.2 The Redefinition: A Year = 12 Weeks

**"A year is no longer 12 months. It's 12 weeks."**

The book borrows **periodization** from sports training: athletes don't train generically all year; they compress intense, targeted focus into short blocks (e.g., spring training), each producing a performance peak. The 12 Week Year applies this to work and life:

- Each **12-week period is a complete "year"**: it has its own goals, its own plan, its own deadline, its own review.
- 12 weeks is the sweet spot: **short enough to create urgency** (the deadline is always near, every day counts), **long enough to achieve meaningful results** (big goals are still possible).
- At the end of each 12 weeks you **celebrate, recover, review, and reload** — then start the next "year" re-energized. You get **four "New Years" per calendar year** instead of one.

### 1.3 Why 12-Week Plans Beat Annual Plans (author's arguments)

1. **More predictable.** The further out a plan reaches, the more guesswork it contains. 12 weeks is close enough to forecast realistically (world, market, and life change less in 12 weeks than in 12 months).
2. **More focused.** A compressed deadline forces you to pick only the critical few objectives, instead of a wish list.
3. **More structured.** The system attaches daily and weekly actions directly to the deadline, so execution starts on day one.
4. **Faster feedback.** You measure weekly and review the full "year" 4x more often than an annual planner — so you correct course in weeks, not years.

### 1.4 The Two Layers: Thinking Model & Doing Model

The book separates the **head** from the **heart** of performance:

- **The thinking model (the head / system):** Vision → Plan → Process Control → Measurement. The structural machinery that directs action.
- **The doing model (the heart / beliefs):** the authors emphasize that beliefs and emotional commitment influence action. Vision is intended to make a future feel meaningful and attainable. Neuroscience comparisons about visualization and action need their own primary evidence; this reference does not present them as established fact.

**Takeaway for the app:** a planning tool that only manages tasks (head) misses half the system; it must also surface the user's *why* (vision, goal reasons, commitments) at the moments of execution and scoring.

## 2. The Execution System: 8 Elements = 5 Disciplines + 3 Principles

The entire methodology is called **The Execution System**, made of **eight elements**:

### The 5 Disciplines (the mechanics)
1. **Vision** — a compelling picture of the future that makes action worth it
2. **Planning** — translating vision into a concrete 12-week plan of goals and tactics
3. **Process Control** — tools and events (weekly plan, scoreboards, accountability meetings) that keep daily actions aligned with the plan
4. **Measurement** — lead and lag indicators plus the weekly execution score
5. **Time Use** — deliberately structuring time (time blocking) instead of letting the day happen

### The 3 Principles (the mindset)
6. **Accountability** — ownership of outcomes, regardless of circumstances
7. **Commitment** — keeping the promises you make to yourself, especially when inconvenient
8. **Greatness in the Moment** — greatness is a series of present-moment choices, not a future event

**The authors describe this as a *closed system*.** The elements are meant to reinforce one another: vision gives direction, planning identifies actions, process control supports execution, measurement enables correction, and time use protects the work. This is the authors' model; the practical effect of adopting only some parts depends on the person and context.

## 3. Discipline 1 — Vision (The Emotional Engine)

**Vision is the starting point of high performance** because it creates the emotional connection that makes execution worth the discomfort.

### 3.1 The Three Horizons (layered vision)

| Horizon | Timeframe | Content |
|---|---|---|
| **Aspirational / long-term vision** | Not time-bound | What you want to create or become in life; broad and personally meaningful |
| **Three-year vision** | 3 years | Specific personal and professional progress toward the aspirational vision |
| **12-month vision** | Optional | An intermediate annual picture, useful when annual performance matters |

The horizons should connect, but the **12-week plan is separate from the vision horizons**: its goals define near-term outcomes and tactics for the next cycle, in service of the three-year and aspirational visions. The Field Guide explicitly treats the 12-month vision as optional and then introduces 12-week goals and tactics in the plan chapter.

### 3.2 Personal Vision Anchors Business Vision

A business/career vision alone is not motivating enough. The **personal vision is primary** — business goals matter because of what they make possible in your life. Ask "what's the business *for*?" The book instructs articulating wants across life domains, e.g.:

- Life / lifestyle, Family, Career & business, Earnings/finances, Social life & friendships, Health & fitness, Hobbies & personal interests, Legacy/contribution, Spirituality, Community, Time use

### 3.3 How to Write It

- Start with **"what if?" — not "how."** Don't limit the vision by current capabilities or logistics; the plan handles the how.
- Write in the present tense, make it emotionally vivid, make it *yours* (not borrowed status symbols).
- **Share it** with people close to you; **revisit it** regularly (weekly, in planning moments); use it to filter decisions — "does this move me toward my vision?"

### 3.4 The Four Vision Pitfalls

1. **Not taking vision seriously** (treating it as a fluffy exercise)
2. **Not personally meaningful** (someone else's dream)
3. **Too small** (uninspiring — if it doesn't slightly scare/excite you, raise it)
4. **Not linked to daily actions** (no bridge from the dream to this week — this is what the 12-week plan exists to fix)

## 4. Discipline 2 — Planning (The 12-Week Plan) ★ The Structural Core

This is the heart of the system's structure. The cascade is:

```
Long-term & 3-year Vision
        ↓ (drives)
12-Week Goals (1–3, rarely more)
        ↓ (are achieved through)
Tactics (concrete, dated, repeated actions — written once, in the 12-Week Plan)
        ↓ (are pulled into)
Weekly Plan (this week's tactics + commitments)
        ↓ (become)
Daily Actions (executed and scored)
```

### 4.1 The 12-Week Plan Document

The plan is a short, focused document containing, per goal:

1. **The Goal** — a specific, measurable 12-week outcome, with a deadline (end of week 12 by default)
2. **Why it matters** — 1–2 sentences connecting it to the vision (emotional fuel when motivation dips)
3. **Tactics** — the vital few actions that *produce* the goal, each with a week assignment

**How many goals?** The book's guidance: **a small number — 2–3 goals per 12-week period for an individual** (never a long list; some organizational adaptations allow up to 3–5). The fewer, the more focused. Every goal must **advance the long-term vision** — if it doesn't, it's someone else's priority and a burnout risk.

**Brainstorm → prioritize → cut.** For each goal, brainstorm every possible tactic, then keep only the *vital few* highest-impact ones. A plan full of everything is a plan of nothing.

### 4.2 Goal Quality Criteria

Each 12-week goal must be:

1. **Specific** — unambiguous, concrete
2. **Measurable / quantified** — a number (calls, pounds, dollars, pages, revenue). Vague goals can't be scored or celebrated
3. **Stated positively** — frame as what you *want* ("98% accurate," not "2% error rate")
4. **A realistic stretch** — ambitious enough to require growth, achievable enough to be credible. If a goal feels easy, raise the bar
5. **Accountable** — a named owner (you, or a person for team plans)
6. **Time-bound** — deadline within the 12 weeks

**Examples of well-formed goals:**
- ❌ "Get in shape" → ✅ "Lose 10 lbs (180 → 170) by the end of week 12"
- ❌ "Grow the business" → ✅ "Add $12,000 of new revenue by end of week 12"
- ❌ "Write more" → ✅ "Complete the 40,000-word first draft of my book by week 12"

### 4.3 Tactics (the plan's engine)

**A tactic is a specific, concrete action, written as a full sentence starting with an action verb, that is executed daily or weekly, and that directly produces its goal.**

Rules for tactics:

- **Format:** complete sentence, verb-first, concrete and countable. ✅ "Perform 30 minutes of strength training 3 times per week." ✅ "Write and publish 2 articles per week." ✅ "Make 10 prospecting calls per week (weeks 2–12)."
- **Week assignment:** every tactic carries a due structure — either **"every week"** (recurring through the cycle) or **specific week(s)** (e.g., "Launch landing page — week 4," "File taxes — week 9"). This is what makes weekly planning mechanical: the plan already says what is due each week.
- **Daily-biased:** prefer tactics that happen daily or several times per week. Daily actions compound and keep the goal alive; the author stresses that *daily* actions determine future results.
- **Strategy-based, not busywork:** every tactic must trace to a goal. Random good ideas do not enter the weekly plan directly — they must be added to the 12-Week Plan first (a deliberate revision), otherwise the plan decays into an urgent to-do list.
- **Don't over-engineer:** "a plan reduces mistakes" and saves time, but planning is preparation, not productivity ("like sharpening your knives"). Adjust the plan mid-cycle as reality teaches you — keep executing.

### 4.4 Worked Example (useful as demo/seed data for the app)

**12-Week Plan — Cycle starting Oct 6**

**Goal 1:** Lose 10 lbs, from 180 to 170, by end of week 12. *(Why: energy and confidence for the 3-year health vision.)*
| # | Tactic | Due |
|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | Log all meals in the food app, every day | Every week |
| 1.2 | Perform 30-min strength training session | 3× per week, every week |
| 1.3 | Walk 8,000 steps | 5× per week, every week |
| 1.4 | Weekly weigh-in and record weight | Every Monday, every week |

**Goal 2:** Earn first $1,000 from the freelance side business by end of week 12. *(Why: prove the income path toward the 3-year independence vision.)*
| # | Tactic | Due |
|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Publish one portfolio article on the blog | Every week |
| 2.2 | Send 10 personalized outreach messages to prospects | Every week, weeks 2–12 |
| 2.3 | Complete and publish service landing page | Week 3 |
| 2.4 | Deliver first paid engagement and collect testimonial | Weeks 6–10 |

Lag indicators: weight (goal 1), revenue (goal 2). Lead indicators: workouts logged, meals logged, articles published, outreach messages sent — and above all the weekly **execution score** (§6).

## 5. Discipline 3 — Process Control (Weekly Rhythm + Peer Support)

Process control = the tools and events that keep daily actions locked to the plan when life gets noisy. Two structures: **the weekly plan** and **peer support (the WAM)**.

### 5.1 The Weekly Plan

- **Not a copy-paste and not an inbox.** Each week it is rebuilt from the 12-Week Plan: pull the tactics due this week ("every week" items + items assigned to this week number), place them on specific days, and add time-bound commitments.
- **Strategic content only.** Urgent-but-unimportant tasks live in buffer blocks (§7), not in the weekly plan.
- **Time cost:** ~**15–20 minutes once a week** to review + plan the week; **~5 minutes each morning** to confirm/refine the day.
- The cascade it enforces: *daily actions → weekly plan → 12-week goals → vision*. If a daily action doesn't ladder up, question it.

### 5.2 The Weekly Routine (the loop that runs the system)

Every week, the same three beats:

1. **Score** last week — compute the execution score (§6), update lag indicators
2. **Plan** the coming week — rebuild the weekly plan from the 12-Week Plan
3. **Attend the WAM** — the accountability meeting (below)

### 5.3 The Weekly Accountability Meeting (WAM)

- A **weekly, standing meeting with 2–4 people** (peers, colleagues, friends — anyone genuinely invested; can also be a coach or group). Its tone is **supportive, not punitive** — it exists to help each other win, not to shame.
- **Typical agenda (per the book's description):**
  1. Individual report-outs: results to date, last week's execution score, intentions for the coming week
  2. Group feedback and problem-solving (help remove blockers)
  3. Share techniques that are working
  4. Encouragement — close on commitment
- It creates **commitment devices**: public commitment, peer expectation, and regular cadence. Skipped weeks are how execution quietly dies; the WAM makes the week itself a deadline.

## 6. Discipline 4 — Measurement (Keep Score; Confront the Truth)

### 6.1 Two Kinds of Measures

| | **Lag indicators** | **Lead indicators** |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | The end results | The activities that produce the results |
| Control | You **cannot** manage them directly | You **can** control them directly |
| Examples | Revenue, pounds lost, book finished | Sales calls made, calories logged, words written, workouts done |
| Cadence | Track against the 12-week goal | Track weekly |

**The single most important lead indicator is execution itself** — whether you did the tactics you committed to. You can't directly control results, but you can fully control actions; actions reliably produce results over time.

### 6.2 The Execution Score (the system's signature metric)

**Formula, computed weekly:**

```
Execution Score % = (tactics completed this week) ÷ (tactics due this week) × 100
```

- **85% is the system's benchmark.** Treat it as a planning and execution guideline from the authors, not a statistically validated probability of reaching a goal. The bands below 85% used by this app are product heuristics; they are not evidence that a person will or will not achieve an outcome.
- Aim for **consistency, not perfection** — nobody scores 100 every week; sustained 85+ wins.
- Scorekeeping measures **only achievements**: "It is not concerned with effort or intentions." No partial credit for trying hard.

### 6.3 Confront the Truth / Productive Tension

- The score is a **mirror**, not a judge. Measuring weekly forces you to *confront the truth* about your execution — most people overestimate their own follow-through, and the data breaks that illusion.
- The discomfort this creates is **productive tension**: the gap between where you are and where you committed to be is exactly what fuels corrective action. Hide the score and the system dies.
- Frequent measurement can help **investigate the failure type**: high execution with weak lag results may point toward the strategy, while low execution may point toward execution barriers. These comparisons are prompts to review the evidence, not diagnoses from one score or a guarantee that the cause is known.

## 7. Discipline 5 — Time Use (Intentionality & Time Blocking)

**Premise:** time is **inelastic** (you can't make more) and **perishable** (unused time is gone forever). Therefore high performers don't "find time" — they **allocate it intentionally**. And **busy ≠ productive**: "being busy is a form of laziness — lazy thinking and indiscriminate action" (Tim Ferriss, cited in the book).

The tool is **time blocking**, installed via a **model work week** (a standing weekly template). Three block types:

### 7.1 The Three Block Types

| Block | Amount | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Strategic Block** | Commonly described as a substantial, uninterrupted block (this reference uses 3 hours) | Your most important work: executing 12-Week Plan tactics. Treat the duration as a recommended template that may need to fit the person's role and schedule. |
| **Buffer Blocks** | **30–60 min per day** (e.g., 11:00–12:00 and/or 4:00–5:00) | Containment for the unavoidable low-value noise: email, voicemail, admin, interruptions. Batching them here keeps them from shredding the whole day. |
| **Breakout Block** | A substantial block away from work (this reference uses 3+ hours) | Full disengagement — family, hobby, nature, rest. The duration is a recommendation, not a guarantee against burnout or a universal requirement. |

### 7.2 The Model Week

- Design a **recurring weekly template** (the "model work week") with the blocks pre-placed — strategic block protected at a fixed slot, buffer blocks at known noise hours, breakout blocks reserved.
- A common pattern from the book's routine: **15 minutes Monday morning** (or end of prior week) for the weekly review + weekly plan, daily 5-minute mornings for the day.
- The weekly accountability meeting also gets a fixed slot in the model week.

## 8. Principle 1 — Accountability (Ownership, Not Consequences)

- **"Accountability is not consequences — it's ownership."** It is *not* punishment, review, or pressure applied by others; it is a **character trait**: the willingness to own your actions and results **regardless of circumstances**.
- **"You cannot hold someone accountable. It's a myth."** External enforcement produces compliance at best. True accountability is *chosen from within* — which is why the system is built on self-scoring and voluntary peer meetings.
- **Language shift:** replace *"I have to"* with *"I choose to."* Same situation, different mindset, different energy, different results.
- **Never the victim:** skip self-pity and excuses; when something fails, the accountability questions are *"What can *I* do next? What action do I own here?"* Failures are **feedback**, not verdicts.
- Spend time with accountable people; accountability is contagious in both directions.

---

## 9. Principle 2 — Commitment (vs. Interest)

- **Interest** = acting when convenient, when you feel like it, with a built-in excuse ("I *tried*").
- **Commitment** = a promise you keep **especially when it's inconvenient or uncomfortable** — no excuses, no negotiating with yourself.
- Commitments in this system are **bounded to 12 weeks** — that's precisely what makes full commitment psychologically possible. You're not promising forever; you're promising 12 weeks. (This is a core reason the 12-week unit works.)

### 9.1 The Four Keys to Commitment (as laid out in the book)

1. **A strong desire** — the goal must connect to something you genuinely want (vision does this)
2. **Keystone actions** — identify the few actions that, done consistently, produce disproportionate results, and protect them
3. **Count the costs** — be honest up front about what the commitment *costs*: comfort, pleasure, some opportunities, some approval. Choose commitments whose costs you can actually pay. (Hidden competing desires — comfort, ease, approval — are what quietly break commitments)
4. **Act on your commitments, not your feelings** — "Learning to do the things you need to do, **regardless of how you feel**, is a core discipline for success." Feelings are weather; commitments are architecture.

Supporting practices: learn to **say no**; **delay gratification**; **go all in** on the vital few rather than hedging across many.

---

## 10. Principle 3 — Greatness in the Moment

- **Greatness is not an event you achieve; it's a choice you make in each moment.** "Results are not the attainment of greatness, but simply confirmation of it." You don't *become* great when the result lands — you *were* great in the accumulated ordinary moments (the workout, the call, the writing session) that produced it.
- Stop waiting to "feel ready" or for the big break: act greatly **now**, with what you control today.
- Presence matters: "In our efforts to not miss anything, we unwittingly miss everything." Focused execution beats scattered availability.
- Illustrations used in the book: champions behave like champions *before* the title (Mike Tyson: "if you don't think you're the champ, you'll never be the champ" — you must *be* it in daily choices); "overnight successes" (Messi cited) are 10+ years of unglamorous moments.

## 11. Intentional Imbalance (Balance Is a Myth)

- **"Life balance is an illusion."** Trying to give every area equal attention every week produces mediocrity everywhere and stress always.
- Instead, practice **intentional imbalance**: at any given season, deliberately over-invest in the few areas that matter most now (typically: the current 12-week goals + health + key relationships), consciously under-serve the rest, **without guilt**.
- The catch: it must be **intentional and reviewed** — each cycle (week 13 review, §13) you check where you're imbalanced and adjust the next 12 weeks accordingly, so neglect never silently becomes permanent.

## 12. The Emotional Cycle of Change (Expect the Dip)

The book presents an emotional cycle of change to help readers anticipate that enthusiasm may fall as the effort becomes real. The stages below summarize that model; the exact timing and sequence should not be treated as a universal empirical schedule:

1. **Uninformed optimism** — excitement about imagined benefits; you start, riding on novelty
2. **Informed pessimism** — reality arrives; the work is harder/slower than imagined; negative emotions grow
3. **Valley of despair** — a period when progress may feel invisible while the costs are clear. The book warns that people may quit here; commitment, peer support, and vision are offered as supports.
4. **Informed optimism** — skills build, benefits start appearing; confidence returns, now grounded in evidence
5. **Success and fulfillment** — the change pays off; the new behavior becomes identity

**App-specific suggestion:** the app may offer a check-in during the middle of a cycle, but a fixed week-3-to-6 warning is a design heuristic, not a prediction about every user's emotions.

## 13. Week 13 — The Review & Reload Period

Each cycle is 12 execution weeks + a **13th week** used to close out and restart:

- **Celebrate and recover** — deliberately honor what was accomplished (most people skip straight to the next grind, which starves motivation)
- **Run the 12-Week Review** (the book prescribes roughly a **2-hour structured review**) — four parts:
  1. **Results** — grade each goal (1–10, or % complete when measurable); review the average weekly execution scores (aim 80–85%+); what worked, what didn't, and *why*
  2. **Quality of Life (intentional imbalance check)** — rate 1–10 across life areas: **Spiritual, Spouse/key relationship, Family, Business/career, Personal, Physical** (define what 1 and 10 mean for each); decide where next cycle's imbalance should tilt
  3. **Success Disciplines** — rate 1–10 your own execution of the system: Vision, 12-week planning, Weekly routine (score/plan/WAM), Performance time (time blocking), Key measures
  4. **Breakthroughs** — identify the cycle's biggest wins; then ask: *how must my thinking change to 2× my results? what new actions are required?* Breakthroughs come from **working *on* the system, not just *in* it** (changing what/how you work on) — not from brute-forcing more hours
- **Build the next 12-Week Plan** while lessons are fresh, then launch the new cycle.

## 14. Getting Started: Your First 12 Weeks (order of operations)

The book's implementation sequence (this is the flow an app onboarding should mirror):

1. **Write the vision** — long-term + 3-year horizons first, personally meaningful, shared
2. **Set 2–3 twelve-week goals** that advance that vision (apply the goal criteria §4.2; write the *why* for each)
3. **Build the 12-Week Plan** — brainstorm tactics per goal, cut to the vital few, assign week dues ("every week" or week N)
4. **Design the model week** — place the strategic block, buffer blocks, breakout blocks, weekly planning slot, WAM slot
5. **Run the weekly loop** — score → plan → WAM; execute daily; keep the execution score visible
6. **Expect resistance may arise** during a change; the often-repeated weeks 3–6 timing is an app/reference heuristic, not a fixed schedule established for every person
7. **Week 13** — review, celebrate, adjust imbalance, build next plan
8. **Commit fully — don't dabble.** The first cycle is the hardest (new habits, new honesty) and the most important; partial adoption of the system is the most common failure mode because the elements are interdependent.

## 15. Chapter-by-Chapter Lessons

The chapter titles below match the published 2013 table of contents. The lesson notes are concise paraphrases synthesized from this reference and checked against the available book/Field Guide contents; they are not chapter-by-chapter quotations or a replacement for the full book.

### Part I — Unleash Your Greatness

1. **The Challenge** — The central challenge is the knowing-doing gap. The practical question is how to make important actions happen consistently, not how to collect more advice.
2. **Redefining the Year** — Treat 12 weeks as a complete execution cycle to make time feel consequential and create earlier feedback. The cycle is a planning device, not a promise to compress a year's worth of work into three months.
3. **The Emotional Connection** — Connect goals to a personally meaningful vision so the reason for acting remains available when effort becomes uncomfortable.
4. **Throw Out the Annual Plan** — Annual plans can postpone urgency and collect too many objectives. Replace the execution plan with a focused set of near-term goals while keeping a longer vision for direction.
5. **One Week at a Time** — Translate the 12-week plan into a weekly plan containing the tactics due now. Use it to choose and schedule the week's critical work rather than to collect every task.
6. **Confronting the Truth** — Measure what was completed and compare it with the plan. Use the gap as information for adjustment; effort and intention are not the same as completion.
7. **Intentionality** — Decide in advance what deserves time, what can wait, and what should be declined. Time blocking and a model week are tools for protecting priority work.
8. **Accountability as Ownership** — Take ownership of one's choices and next actions instead of relying on blame or external pressure. Ownership means asking what can be done now, while recognizing that not every circumstance is controllable.
9. **Interest versus Commitment** — Interest depends on convenience and mood; commitment is a deliberate promise that can guide action when motivation fluctuates.
10. **Greatness in the Moment** — Excellence is practiced through present choices and repeated actions; the result later reflects the quality of those moments.
11. **Intentional Imbalance** — Equal attention to every life area at every moment is unrealistic. Choose priorities deliberately for a season and review the trade-offs so temporary focus does not become permanent neglect.

### Part II — Putting It All Together

12. **The Execution System** — Combine the five disciplines (vision, planning, process control, measurement, time use) with the three principles (accountability, commitment, greatness in the moment). The chapter also introduces the emotional cycle of change and describes the elements as a closed system.
13. **Establish Your Vision** — Write an aspirational vision and concrete intermediate visions, then use them to guide cycle goals. The Field Guide identifies a three-year personal/business vision and an optional 12-month vision; 12-week goals belong to the plan, not to the vision horizons.
14. **Develop Your 12 Week Plan** — Set specific, measurable 12-week outcomes that advance the vision. Identify the critical few tactics for each goal and assign when they are due; keep the plan simple enough to execute.
15. **Installing Process Control** — Make execution repeatable with weekly plans, peer support and a weekly routine. The related Field Guide also describes practices such as daily huddles, cycle themes and celebrations.
16. **Keeping Score** — Track both execution (lead actions / tactics completed) and outcomes (lag measures / results). These are complementary views: the execution score measures adherence to planned actions, while lag measures show whether the intended results are moving. The book's 85% mark is a benchmark, not a statistical guarantee; it does not define a single blended process-plus-outcome score.
17. **Take Back Control of Your Day** — Protect focused performance time, batch interruptions into buffer time, and schedule time away from work. Model the week as a guide and adapt the blocks to the person's real obligations.
18. **Taking Ownership** — Turn the accountability principle into daily choices and actions. Notice excuses or victim thinking, then identify the next action that is genuinely within one's control.
19. **12 Week Commitments** — Consider what a commitment means for oneself and for other people, what it will cost, and what actions will honor it. A short cycle makes the promise bounded enough to review and renew.
20. **Your First 12 Weeks** — Start with a plan, then work through the cycle's early enthusiasm, middle resistance and final execution. The chapter addresses common barriers such as immediate gratification, too many goals and old habits, and leads into the 13th-week review.
21. **Final Thoughts and the 13th Week** — Close the cycle by recognizing progress, reviewing results and execution, learning from the experience, and preparing the next plan. Week 13 is a review/reload period, separate from the 12 scored execution weeks.

**Interpretation boundary:** chapter lessons summarize the method. The database entities, UI flows, thresholds, notifications, and app invariants in §18 are product design proposals derived from the method, not requirements stated by the authors.

## 16. Key Numbers & Rules Cheat Sheet

| Item | Value / Rule |
|---|---|
| Length of a "year" | **12 weeks** (+ week 13 for review/reload) |
| Goals per cycle | **2–3** (rarely more; every goal must trace to vision) |
| Execution score formula | completed ÷ due tactics, per week |
| Execution score benchmark | **85%+** is the authors' benchmark; lower score bands and “revise” prompts are app heuristics, not outcome predictions |
| Weekly planning time | **15–20 min/week** + **5 min/day** |
| Strategic block | **3 h/week**, uninterrupted, plan-tactic work |
| Buffer blocks | **30–60 min/day** for email/admin noise |
| Breakout block | **3+ h/week** fully away from work |
| WAM | Weekly peer accountability; group size is a practical implementation choice |
| Weekly loop | Score → Plan next week → WAM |
| Week 13 | Celebrate + ~2 h structured review + build next plan |
| Vision layers | Aspirational (not time-bound) → three-year → optional 12-month; 12-week goals are a separate plan |
| Quality-of-life areas | Spiritual, Spouse/key relationship, Family, Business, Personal, Physical |
| Emotional dip | The book's change model includes a “valley of despair”; exact timing varies and should not be presented as a fixed 3–6 week prediction |

## 18. Mapping to a Planning Web App (derived guidance for the build agent)

This section is *derived from* the book's system (not the author's words) — a suggested blueprint so the app implements the methodology faithfully.

### 18.1 Core entities / data model

- **Vision** — `longTermStatement`, `threeYearStatement`, `perCycleConnection` (why this cycle matters), life-domain notes; editable each cycle, versioned per cycle
- **Cycle** — `startDate`, 12 execution weeks + flag for **week 13**; `status` (planning / active / review); auto "New Year" restart
- **Goal** (per cycle, enforce **max ~3**, warn beyond) — `description` (validated against criteria: measurable, positive, stretch, deadline), `why` (required field), `lagIndicator {metric, startValue, targetValue, unit}`, `owner`
- **Tactic** (belongs to goal) — `text` (verb-first sentence), `duePattern`: `EVERY_WEEK {timesPerWeek}` or `SPECIFIC_WEEKS [n…]}`, `completedThisWeek` boolean per week (completion log per week number)
- **WeekPlan** (per cycle-week) — auto-generated proposal from tactics due that week (user places on days), plus time-bound commitments; rebuilt fresh each week (never copy-paste)
- **DailyAction** — the day-placed instance of a tactic; checkbox; feeds scoring
- **ExecutionScore** (per week) — computed: `Σ tactic completions ÷ Σ tactics due × 100`; shown against the **85%** line; trend chart across the cycle
- **LeadMeasure** — optional per goal (e.g., calls made), tracked weekly
- **TimeBlockTemplate** ("model week") — recurring blocks typed `STRATEGIC (3h) | BUFFER (30–60m) | BREAKOUT (3h+)` + weekly planning slot + WAM slot
- **WAM** — per week: attendees, per-person report (results, score, next-week intentions), blockers, encouragement notes; exportable agenda
- **CycleReview** (week 13 wizard) — 4 steps: Results (goal grades, avg score), Quality of Life (6 areas × 1–10 sliders), Success Disciplines (5 items × 1–10), Breakthroughs (thinking changes + new actions); then "Create next cycle" flow carrying vision forward

### 18.2 Feature list implied by the book

1. **Cycle dashboard**: current week #, days left in "year", goals with lag progress, execution-score trend vs 85%
2. **Vision page** always one click away — surfaced during planning, scoring, and the valley weeks (the *why* must be visible at moments of execution)
3. **Plan builder wizard**: vision → goals (with quality validation) → tactics (with due-pattern helper) → model week
4. **Weekly loop screen**: "Score last week → Plan this week → (WAM checklist)" — the app's home every Monday
5. **Daily execution checklist** (5-minute morning view) with only today's actions
6. **Auto execution score** + honest feedback copy ("The score measures achievements, not effort or intentions")
7. **Valley-of-despair guardrail**: weeks 3–6 nudges with commitment reminders, vision re-surface, WAM prompts (from the emotional cycle of change)
8. **Week 13 mode**: celebration summary first, then the 4-part review wizard, then next-cycle planning; cycle history archive
9. **Plan-change discipline**: adding ad-hoc tasks to a week routes through "add to 12-Week Plan?" confirmation — keeping weekly plans strategic
10. **Scoring feedback loop**: if avg score < ~70% for 2+ weeks → suggest revising tactics (per the book: revise the plan rather than keep failing)

### 18.3 Invariants the app should enforce (fidelity to the book)

- A year = 12 weeks; week 13 is review, not execution
- Max 3 goals/cycle (soft limit with warning)
- Every tactic belongs to a goal; every goal has a `why` and a measurable lag target
- Execution score counts **completions only** — no effort/partial credit
- 85% displayed as the benchmark on all scoring UI
- Weekly plan regenerates from the 12-Week Plan each week (no copy-paste)
- Strategic/buffer/breakout blocks present in the model week before the cycle can start

## 19. Reader Feedback and Research Sources

### 19.1 Reader and practitioner feedback (anecdotal)

Public forum posts and independent reviews describe both benefits and friction. Some users say the short cycle and weekly check-ins help them keep goals visible and build focus. Others report that setup and tracking take discipline, that it is easy to over-track or keep recalibrating, and that the system can feel rigid when work or life needs flexibility. One user described retaining weekly check-ins while separating habit-like trackables from flexible project tasks. These reports are useful as design signals, but they are self-selected anecdotes, not representative user research or evidence that the method causes better outcomes.

An independent review frames the system as most useful when the goal is already clear and execution is the main problem; it cautions that exploratory work may be forced into premature weekly tactics and that the process can feel repetitive for people already using quarterly planning. Treat these as reviewer opinions, not findings established by controlled studies.
